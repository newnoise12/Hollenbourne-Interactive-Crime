## Founding description of Hollenbourne, and the original suspect list

Hollenbourne, as we've said is a place of two halves and the local council have worked hard in recent years to see investment. The high rates of criminality in the area is a constant concern and many feel as if it drives away would be residents to places such as the Featherton estate. This area of new build residential properties is situated just behind Hollenbourne high street, near to the supermarket and railway station. The council hopes that professionals and young families will be drawn to the area, allowing more income to flow into the local economy. At present there are around 85,000 residents in Hollenbourne and its surrounding area and new housing projects aim to replace existing housing around the Critchley plant and provide space for 20,000 new residents by 2032. The tenement buildings around the Critchley plant are post war tower blocks in need of much repair. Some buildings have been emptied out but others are still inhabited by low income families and retired people. It is a poor area and, like the Boresfield estate, it is associated with the county lines drug trade.

## Martin Burgess: biography and the killing of Geoff Mason

Grows up poor in low income family, abusive father, witnesses domestic violence throughout his childhood. Works to leave home as quickly as possible. Does trade apprenticeships and eventually trains to be a boiler technician. Has a history of delinquency as a teenager. Withdrawn at school but occasionally has the capacity for extreme violence. Sense of very strong regulation of emotions which occasionally gives way and leads to violent outbursts. Tried for manslaughter in early adulthood but case collapses due to insufficient evidence. Altercation at a nightclub, no direct cctv footage of the event is available to jury, we don't need tons of detail on this, but what's important is that it leaves him with a sense of impunity.

As he grows older he has a few short term relationships but they tend to end in arguments and police have sometimes been called. No charges ever pressed. At the present time, Burgess is living alone in a town near Hollenbourne, he enjoys a solitary life with few friends. It is ambiguous whether mason is his first victim, there could be more. Especially given that the details of the manslaughter are unknown. Certainly something changes when he encounters Mason. At the time Burgess' father has recently died and there are a lot of complex feelings and unresolved issues there. Mason does something which reminds him of his father and Burgess has an almost instinctive response. He pushes Mason around and hits him in the mouth. Worrying for his job and still angry and aroused, he takes a wrench and hits mason 4-5 more times on his head and neck. This is the messiest and most improvised of the murders. Burgess has his gloves on as he's been working. He goes to his car for a tarpaulen to cover mason's body, opens the old man's back door and drags him out. It's around 3:45 on the 8th october, its getting dark but it isn't dark yet. Mason takes the body in the tarp to the nearby marshes. To an onlooker he is a tradesperson disposing of some buillding materials but there are very few onlookers. He finds a secluded area and dumps the body. He returns to the house closing the back door, locking it, and shakily completes the boiler service he was there to do, dating it at the time he would have finished had he not killed the occupant. He leaves through the front door, making sure to say 'goodbye, take care' as he leaves and drives home in his work van. As he drives home he begins to worry about the tarpaulen which would be linked back to him. He goes home and then calls his mother to ask if he can borrow his father's car, a black 4x4. He waits for dark and arrives at hollen marsh carpark at 9:30 pm. Under the cover of dark he finds the body and takes the tarp. Realising he has left two sets of tracks in the soft ground he leaves the area via a different route, eventually finding himself in a secluded residential area where he can walk along a pavement back to his father's car. Exhausted, he drives back to his home, dropping the car off at his mother's and walking back to his house. He his emotional when he is back at home but not in the way that he imagined. He is not disgusted at himself, he is not horrified by the blood, or at the horror of taking someone's life. He has crossed a line and the capacity to feel guilt has been shut off. While he is concerned about being caught and anxious about any evidence he'd left in the house, there is a quiet sense of satisfaction that he gave his father what he deserved, albeit through the symbolic presence of Geoff Mason. He feels powerful, he has acted out something he never could have done when he was a scared child.

## Aftermath: the October 2019 interview, and the years to Wooley

Burgess doesn't know if he's done enough to remove himself from suspicion. In fact he hasn't, the police investigators were ambivalent about him, finding him somewhat inauthentic at points. At one point in the interview he refers to Geoff as 'Rich', his fathers name. Officers found his description of checking up on Geoff, while he was leaving, as unconvincing, as if Burgess was subtly trying to point them towards the issues of county lines to explain how Mason ended up dying. He later doubled-down on this in a exchange where police asked him straight on whether he had killed Mason.

Interviewer: Mr Burgess, did you have any involvement in the death of Geoff Mason
Burgess: No. Absolutely not, no.
Interviewer: Do you have any idea who did it?
Burgess: No, I don't. All I can think is that its one of those estates, you know? Drugs and that. Kids...
Interviewer: Could you be more specific.
Burgess: I mean you're always hearing about Hollenbourne and county lines stuff. People kicking people out of their houses and selling drugs from there. You know more about it than me.
Interviewer: Did you see anything that made you think that Geoff was involved in that?
Burgess: No not really, but like I said he seemed stressed about something. I wondered if it was that.

Despite the police's concerns the forensic evidence on Mason's body is very limited, since it had been outside for so long. Burgess' DNA was at the scene but nothing that wouldn't be explained by his presence to do a boiler check. The case runs cold as police interview Mason's neighbours who have very little to say about the quiet widower who would ocassionaly be seen walking on the marshland. Certainly, local residents coroborated the presence of county lines operations in the area, but nobody would confirm that mason had been involved in any way.

Burgess' life returns to normal and he hears nothing else about the case. He works from time to time in the Hollenbourne area, and his sense of impunity begins to grow again. Having spoken to the police, he understands that they have nothing on him. The sense that he is the main character in a story grows. When he killed, it seemed like he did everything right. The tarpaulin, taking him to the woods, his story to the police, all worked. He was smarter than them, a lot smarter. Perhaps he was the only smart person in the world? These thoughts grew and grew in his mind, especially during the period in 2020 when the country was locked down during covid. For a year he was on furlough, sitting at home watching television, buying takeaways, speaking only to his mother and occasionally his sister. A solitary man to begin with, Burgess was effectively removed from society completely as his contact with friends and colleagues became zero. Ocassionally he'd wake up feeling angry at everything. Hemmed in, going crazy. He would storm around his small flat, knocking things off the kitchen table and hammering at the wall. On one ocassion, police were called to do a welfare check but when they arrived he simply explained that he had been moving furniture around and had dropped a piece of crockery. A record was made but police left feeling that, whatever the issue was, he'd calmed down and wasn't a threat to himself.

Burgess spent his days ruminating on his past, building a story for himself. He'd turn the tv on but wouldn't watch it, sitting on his sofa dreaming up ways of restoring himself to a place of dignity by writing the wrongs of his childhood, his adolescence, and more and more, his most recent relationships. By mid 2021 he was out working again. He was busy, the pent up demand by landlords and homeowners for boiler repairs and services meant that he was thrown back into life, forced to interact with people suddenly after 8 months of almost complete solitude. He was glad of his company's mask policy, as it allowed him to hide his expressions, keep quiet and do his work. In the past he had gotten quite good at the usual patter, now he came in and left, saying only what was necessary.

At the end 2021 he was in the hollenbourne area almost weekly. The houses were poorly maintained and landlords were often unwilling to replace boilers before they finally broke, leaving renters with no hot water during the winter months. Where Burgess had been sympathetic to the poor residents, forced into substandard accomodation and left at the whims of neglectful landlords, this winter was different. His opinion of Hollenbourne had become disdainful since the death of Mason and in a strange displacement of guilt he blamed the community, the police force, and the local authority for not protecting the old man, or bringing him to justice. Whenever he entered the area he would scowl at the poorly maintained gardens and chipping paint and if anyone had asked him, he would have called the area a septic tank, rammed full of human shit.

When he attended a call at Susan Wooley's house in January 2022 he was disarmed by the well kept lawn, neat flower beds, and none of the garish christmas decorations which often stayed in place all year long on the fronts of some of her neighbours' houses. Wooley's boiler would no longer maintain pressure and its heating was inconsistent. Despite the cold house, she greeted him with warmth, offered him a tea and asked questions about his Christmas. She made interaction so easy, she was easy to talk to. It was like he had met a real human for the first time in a year. At first his interactions were creaky, talking to her was like using a muscle which had been unused for years. If you asked him, Burgess couldn't himself tell you why, after 35 minutes of being in her house, Wooley's blood was pooling around her head as she lay on the floor in her small kitchen. The last thing he remembered was, once again, saying goodbye to a dead body as he left her house. This time no receipt was left, any signs of tampering with the boiler were undone as carefuly as possible. This time Burgess did not want a record of his presence. He walked accross the street to his van and drove on to his next appointment in a daze. Somewhere in the newly build featherton estate, a boiler has been opened and serviced with traces of Wooley's blood transferred through the same tool he used to kill both Wooley and Mason.

## Nigel Wooley: biography

Nigel Wooley had no run-ins with the police untill 2021. He was born in a middle class family in Basildon and studied (at university?) to become a surveyor in 1993. He married his first wife, Pamela, in 1995 and had two children with her. In 2006 the two separated and later divorced. They went through mediation and were able to agree custody arrangements without going to court. Pamela was interviewed by the police after the death of Susan Wooley. Pamela stated that Nigel had never been violent to her or the children, but had worried after they had separated that he had been 'keeping tabs' on her. He would often ask her friends for information about her love life, and Pamela claimed that she had once run into him as she was leaving a restaurant. Given the context she was suspicious that he had been following her.

In 2012 Nigel met Susan Edwards (later Wooley) through a mutual friend. Susan had ended a long term relationship two years ago and was interested in meeting someone else. The two became involved and decided to get married in 2014. Susan had no children and, as a teaching assistant at Hollenbourne Comprehensive, she would often joke she 'had enough children in her life, not to mention Nigel'. While this was always said in a joking spirit, it is clear from the interview with Pamela and Susan's friend Nadia (who had introduced the two) that Nigel had certain insecurities which would often come out in defensiveness and combatativeness. There was a sense in which 'he had to win' according to Pamela, and would often withdraw from arguments or conversations if he felt they weren't going his way. Pamela described this as a kind of childish sulkiness which she had thought he would grow out of.

Susan had described her frustrations about this behaviour with other friends and would sometimes allude to having 'rushed into' the relationship (Diane - Friend). In 2021, after a tense and bruising lockdown, which placed a great deal of pressure on them both, Susan ended the relationship, separating from Nigel. As Susan owned the house in which they resided in the Boresfield estate, Nigel moved out to the Featherton Estate. However, Nigels controlling tendencies returned with force and Susan reported him to the police for stalking later that year. Susan described receiving hundreds of text messages a day in the early stages of their break-up. And after blocking his number, Nigel would show up at her house. The persistence felt unhinged and unreasonable, and the courts placed a restraining order on Nigel, preventing him from entering into the Boresfield estate.

Nigel's neighbours in his appartment building described him as a quiet but intense man, unapproachable and untalkative. At the time of Susan's death he had breached his restraining order once but told his probation officer that he was just walking through the estate to get to the marshes to walk his dog. He was warned at this point that he would be facing a custodial sentence if he was found to have broken his order again.

## The killing of Sara Butt

The blackout affects Burgess. He manages to get through his day and collapses when he gets home. This feels different. Both murders were unplanned, but this one was totally out of his control. He is wracked with anxiety, he follows the news obsessively, he monitors local facebook discussion groups. He knows the date of Wooley's funeral, he pieces together parts of her life from these discussions, and whatever information is availablle about her online. He doesn't feel guilt, per se, but he does feel as if he's made a mistake. Perhaps a moral mistake, certainly a practical one. He doesn't remember what happened to her, he doesn't remember how he killed her, except that when he got to his next appointment, his spanner was slick with darkening blood and he almost vomited into his mask from the feeling of stress. If the homeowners were interviewed, they would have told the police that burgess was sweating, short tempered, and hurried to finish the repairs. They would have said that when they came into the room to check how he was getting on, he had suddenly stopped and moved his tool box out of their sight before awkwardly describing how a valve needed replacing.

To put it in short, this was a wake-up call for Burgess. A sign that he was slipping up too much, and that his slipping mental health was no longer a strength which allowed him to maintain a fantasy of restoring past-injustices he had suffered and feeling superior to everyone else, but instead a liability. But he was trapped. Asking for professional help was not an option. What if he had to talk about himself and what was going on in his mind? What if they drugged him and he confessed? Would he be put on some kind of register that flagged up with the police, bringing them back into his life? This was a lonely and stressfull time for Burgess. At this time his mother was diagnosed with a terminal illness and being the siblling that lived closest to her, it was expected that he would help out with her care. Despite his mental state, Burgess would see his mother every day, often staying over at her house in the last months of her life in 2023 and taking her to hospital appointments. He woulld see his sister occasionally, and while their relationship had been distant in recent years there were points of reconnection. Now that they were both adults they could talk about their father more honestly, and some of the complexity of their family life came out into the open. In March 2023 Burgess mother died and while he greived her there was a sense of closure about his early life, now that both parents were dead, and new beginnings as his relationship with his sister and her family grew. The years after his mother's death were relativelly stablle for Burgess. He reconnected with a number of friends and begun a relationship in late 2024 with a woman he'd met on a dating app. If the world would tacitly forgive him of his murders by letting him get away with them, then why should he let them hold him back. Hollenbourne was a pile of toxic waste in his mind and the people he killed would have likely been murdered or died from overdoses at some point in time, in his view.

After living with each other in Burgess' flat for 2 months at the beginning of 2025, Burgess and his partner split up. If she were interviewed she would say that he had become detatched, uninterested in her life, and the sense of momentum building in their relationship had ended after a month of living together. He would avoid spending time with her, often undertaking long hours at work or staying on later than she could really understand. Around the house he was often in a daze, but would sometimes get very angry over small things. While she would never say he was violent to her, she would sometimes say she felt scared of him and his temper.

It was clear that the period of stability and personal growth had come to an end for Burgess for some reason. Perhaps it was the responsibility of having a partner, perhaps its was some unprocessed feelings from his mother's death but Burgess returned to a familiar pattern of thought, and after his breakup a renewed desire for retribution had been ignited. He had had the odd job in Hollenbourne over the past few months but there was nothing on the books. But somehow he found himself heading back there. Perhaps it was a place that he had decided was where his emotions could fullly take form, perhaps he thought that he could outsmart the police there, perhaps he felt as if the town itself deserved a taste of his justice for the crimes that others carried out there. Either way, he found himself on the evening of May 22nd taking a bus to the Critchley estate and taking a late evening walk through the backstreets behind Hollenbourne high street, around the featherton estate where surveillance was dense, and around into the marshes where he had dumped Mason's body all those years before. Was he there to murder? He didn't know. He found some kind of pleasure in scornfully walking through the area, muttering words like 'trash' or 'filth' under his breath as he passed locals. Was he operating as if he wanted to commit a murder? Yes, either by instinct or some desire that he had yet to fully articulate to himself, he found himself on that bleak marshland, sitting near the where mason had been found observing a bus stop near the carpark, out of view, waiting for something to make a decision for him. It came at 10:49. The bus stops, a young woman, Sara Butt, exits. He needs to be quick, there are no connecting busses from this stop and the lit, residential backstreets of the Boresfielld estate is only a 2 minute walk. She permits him an opportunity, looking through her bag for a moment, fishing out her phone and sending her last whatsapp message, teling her parents she'll be back in 10. He walks towards her quietly. When he gets to the street she has already crossed over. He has to strike hard and fast. He runs at her. She looks around and manages to yell once before her jaws are locked shut by Burgess' hands. He bundles her back accross the road and into the marshland where, for the first time, he uses his hands to strangle a person to death.

Had Geoff Mason still be alive, he might have heard her scream, as his house was nearest to the marsh. Unfortunately, Sara was too far away from the residential area for anyone to hear. Rather than leaving her body where Mason's was. Burgess decides to take Sara's body to the wet part of the marsh and he does his best to cast her body out so that it will be subsumed by water. The lower half of his trousers and shoes become soaked but he manages to place Sara's body under 2 inches of water. It is 5 days before a dog walker spots her red shirt and calls the police.

## Podcast script — "Raw Crime Podcast" episode, Hollenbourne

*Written by Chris in full, across two drafts. Reproduced here corrected — the errors caught in review (murder count, plant employment years, road name, a real-world university fact, a typo) are fixed rather than preserved, since those are mistakes rather than voice choices. The original uncorrected draft is not kept separately; this is the version to work from.*

This is the Raw Crime Podcast where current, past, and future cases collide. My name is Chris Waller and this week we're looking at a brutal series of killings in Essex in the town of Hollenbourne, recently dubbed the murder capital of the UK. We're going to take a deep dive into the dark recent history of this town and ask ourselves the question which the police are currently asking themselves: are the deaths of Geoff Mason, Susan Wooley, Carl Porterhouse, and Sara Butt, connected? Join us for another Raw Crime Deep-Dive.

Hollenbourne in Essex has been the site of 24 murders since 2018 with an average rate of 3 murders a year. In 2022 5 murders were recorded including the death of Susan Wooley.

A former industrial town near the Thames which serviced London's docklands up until the 1980s, Hollenbourne was best known for the Critchley cement works which employed 3000 workers in the surrounding area. As older residents recalled, the looming towers of the Critchley plant and the thick grey dust which used to cover the houses and shops in the town served as a source of local pride. As Hollenbourne expanded, nearby quarries serving the works with chalk started to become encroached upon, leading to a shortage of easily accessible materials to make the cement. After the economic crises of the 1970's and an increasingly globalised market for building materials, in 1982 the Critchley plant blew its last puffs of dust onto the town who's growth and success had strangled to death the very industry which gave it life.

Looking at Hollenbourne today, it is easy to imagine this puff of dust as the parting shot in a duel between the industrial past and a prosperous future, a shot which did not kill the town off but left it maimed. Today unemployment, drug abuse, homelessness and, of course, violence are ever-present in the area. From a prosperous and hopeful past to a decaying settlement surrounded by water and marshland, Hollenbourne characterises the ravages of de-industrialisation, and a loss of civic connection.

Despite these challenges, attempts have been made in recent years by the local authorities to turn the town around and the Featherton estate in the north east of the town serves as a beacon to a new generation of commuters, young professionals, and wealthy Londoners looking to leave the city. As part of a surge of investment from both government and private sources in 2016 the Featherton estate and its nearby commercial area, dubbed 'the piazza' but known by certain locals by a less salubrious name, were finished in early 2020. While the pandemic slowed the influx of residents, it is hoped that by 2032 Hollenbourne will attract 20,000 new residents and there are future plans to demolish the tenement blocks around the Critchley estate to make space for an Elizabeth line extension and more new and repurposed buildings.

This prospect serves another purpose, as the Critchley estate is notorious for the drugs trade and police have worked tirelessly to combat the persistent extension of county lines dealing from North-East London via the railway and the A13. Of the 24 murders recorded over the past years 14 have been tied directly to the drug trade, and it is speculated that 4 more are connected to varying extents to the tight grip that these gangs have over the area. Of these deaths, 11 occurred in the Critchley estate. But for our discussion today, we must follow the shadow of the Critchley towers a little further along towards the Boresfield estate. A sprawling area in the south of Hollenbourne that borders Hollen Marshes. For residents of Boresfield, the shadow of the Critchley towers is more than a geographic feature and many have complained that the press attention on the Critchley drug trade and its associated violence has served to direct resources away from them, leaving them to suffer from frequent instances of anti-social behaviour, drug-related offending, and growing level of violence. Police sources have, themselves, indicated a growing drug problem in the area and a possibility that the recent violence is linked to territorial competition between county-lines gangs operating in both the Critchley and Boresfield estates.

So what is the point of all this, you now might be asking yourself? Well, I say all this to set a context. A deprived town, desperate to renew itself, to tell a story of hope for the first time since the 1970's. A dark and foreboding estate, dense with deprivation, and those who profit from it. And a region in the south, the Boresfield estate, row after row of terraced houses, desperate for repair, on the verge of a bleak and misty marshland, in which 5 deaths have occurred which don't have an easy explanation.

Geoff Mason, an 88-year-old man, lived right next to the marsh on his own. He grew up in a working class family in Deptford, South London, during the war and remembered bombs falling on London during the blitz. After working with his father throughout his early adulthood he moved to Hollenbourne in 1954 to work in the new cement factory. He met his wife, Doreen, that same year and they married in 1957. They first lived in a small flat in the recently built Howill tower on the Critchley estate then when Doreen became pregnant with their first child in 1958 they bought a house in Boresfield. While Doreen liked the rugged wilderness of Hollen Marsh, Geoff always found it bleak and empty feeling, preferring to cultivate his garden and allotment with bright and alluring produce. They lived together happily, raising two more children till 1982 when the Critchley plant, a source of reliable income for 28 years, closed down. For a few years Geoff worked as a gardener and retired at 60. He would often talk about the pain he felt seeing the plant close down and the massive impact it had on the area. When he moved to Boresfield in the early 60's the estate was a safe, well-kept place in which children would play on the quiet streets. He would often say that 'anyone with any sense moved out in the 1990's'. In 2014 Doreen was diagnosed with dementia and died in 2017. Geoff often blamed the increasing stress of living in the area but found solace in walking on the marshes with Doreen in her later years, following the same route and ending up by the watery places where Canadian geese would nest in the warmer parts of the year.

Geoff continued to walk the marshes in the years after 2017, despite his previous disdain for them, saying that they made him feel closer to Doreen. It was these marshes where, in October 2019, Geoff's body was found. He had suffered deep fractures to his skull inflicted by a blunt object. As an unsolved case, there are details that I don't have access to. What I can say is that police are still unsure as to whether Geoff was killed while he was on an evening walk on the marshes, or whether his body was dumped there. Forensic evidence is inconclusive, given that his body was left outside and only found 3 days after his death in a bushy area by a dog walker. All we can say is that it is very hard to understand why this man, in his golden years, was killed so brutally. Geoff Mason's death did not receive much media coverage outside of the local papers. And while investigators are still assigned to it, to date there has been little movement as far as we know. While Geoff lived a quiet life, he was known to residents of Boresfield who will talk about his death to this day and the fact that his killer remains at large.

We now turn to Susan Wooley who's body was found in her house on Harrison Road on the Boresfield estate, bludgeoned to death with a blunt object. Like Geoff Mason, Susan's death left a lasting impact on the Boresfield community. Surrounded by violence, vandalism, theft, and burglary, the residents know when something inexplicable has occurred and when the news came through that Susan was dead under suspicious circumstances, questions were forthcoming. A well-liked local teaching assistant and frequent volunteer at her local church, found dead in her house, what could have possibly been the reason for this senseless act of violence?

Susan grew up in Tottenham in the 1960's. A bright and ambitious child of a Caribbean family she excelled academically and took a place at Reading university studying Chemistry in 1982. After completing her degree she undertook teacher training and taught in secondary schools across northeast London, earning awards for her work and the support she gave to students. Numerous former students would talk about the difference she made in their lives and the encouragement she gave them to aim high. At her funeral, she was described as an extremely warm and caring person, incredibly skilled at putting people at ease and always easy to talk to. She married her first husband, Daniel Williams who ran a hardware shop, in 1991, and lived with him in Walthamstow. The relationship lasted till 2010 when the two separated and eventually divorced. Wanting a change and to be closer to friends and family members, Susan and Daniel sold their house and Susan bought a house in Hollenbourne where she had recently accepted a job at St Martin's primary school as a teaching assistant. Friends said that she wanted to make space in her life for hobbies and the volunteering work she had done at the church. She became involved with parish life quickly and would talk about the profound levels of need she encountered in Hollenbourne working at the food-bank and befriending elderly members of the parish. With a less stressful role at the school, Susan had more time to socialise and in 2012, a friend set her up with Nigel Wooley, a surveyor from the town who was also recently divorced. The two hit it off and started a relationship, looking forward to retiring together and spending their time travelling. Within a year the two had moved in together to Susan's house on the Boresfield estate and they married each other in 2014. Nigel's two adult children from his previous marriage enjoyed spending time with Susan and felt that she was good for their father, dealing with his occasional childish stubbornness well with her years of experience as a teacher. Having never had children of her own, Susan would sometimes joke saying that she had 'enough children in her life' with her school work and Nigel. After a successful holiday to the United States in 2016 with Nigel and his children, Susan thought that she had found a partner with whom she would spend the rest of her life. Though he could be difficult at times, Nigel was a caring and devoted husband, who would sometimes surprise her with short weekend breaks to attractive villages and European cities. However, by 2018 the cracks in the relationship had begun to show and Nigel's peculiarities and childish strops were beginning to wear on Susan. In conversations with friends, Susan would describe Nigel as 'insecure' and would refer to his need to 'win' the discussions they had, often withdrawing entirely if he felt that the discussion wasn't going his way and remaining silent for days. Susan felt like she was losing her independence and, after the first months of lockdown in 2020, she had decided to separate from him. While the lockdown restrictions placed extreme limitations on the housing market, Nigel was able to find a flat in the newly built Featherton estate and moved out in June of 2020. Nigel took the breakup hard, and would often message Susan, sometimes up to 50 times a day. When Susan blocked his number and made her social media private, Nigel would turn up to her house, demanding that she come out to talk. The police were called on several occasions and in 2021 a restraining order was placed on Nigel, effectively banning him from the Boresfield estate. While Susan put on a brave face at work and in her volunteering, those who knew her well could tell that Nigel's behaviour had unsettled her. Nigel's neighbours in Featherton described him as a quiet but intense man who kept to himself and Susan worried that the time spent alone with his own thoughts during the break-up had caused his mental health to suffer badly. On two occasions after his restraining order, Susan had seen Nigel in the vicinity of her house, walking his dog, and the second time she phoned in to the police. Nigel was warned that any further breaches would result in a custodial sentence.

When, in 2022, Susan was found dead in her house by the police who had been called to do a welfare check, Nigel was a prime suspect. His seeming lack of concern for the consequences of his actions for either Susan or himself lead police to question him. While the details of the interview are unknown, police released him and Susan's killer has still not been found. The community remains devastated by this senseless killing of a woman who's easy-going charm and driving passion to help others touched the lives of so many of them.

In 2022 5 murders were recorded in the Hollenbourne area, including that of Susan. This statistic finally broke through into the national press with the Sun running the headline 'Horrorbourne'. The police, already under pressure from local community groups and the local authority to meaningfully address the violence, were now subject to scrutiny from the national media and politicians. Additional funding was made available to the police in 2023 to tackle the county-lines gangs operating in the area. Collaborating with other forces across the area, inroads were made into dismantling parts of these operations and identifying the 'elders' who ran the operations. Cases were built and gradually arrests led to convictions, of those who had been involved in the violence. But despite this effort, the residents of Boresfield still felt unsafe, and while the police were somewhat more present in the area, the reality of daily life had not meaningfully changed. They knew that the drugs trade continued to operate and when, in 2023, Carl Porterhouse was found bludgeoned to death in his house, the same fear resurfaced in a community that was still coming to terms with the death of Susan Wooley.

While Wooley had been a well-liked and respected member of what little community existed on the Boresfield estate, Porterhouse was a more divisive figure. Having a long criminal history including armed robbery and aggravated assault, Carl was treated with suspicion by those who were acquainted with him. He had served over 10 years in prison and was known as a drug user, becoming addicted to cocaine in the years after his release. He had lived in the surrounding areas of Grays and Romford for much of his life, and would happily tell those around him of his past life in organised crime. Despite his tough past, Porterhouse was known to be a genuine and honest person, who many locals at the Robin Hood pub had affection for. After his release from prison, Carl had worked as a labourer but had suffered a back injury leaving him unable to continue. While he still did odd jobs in the area, it was clear to those who knew him that Carl's physical health was worsening in the years leading up to his death. His drug use was growing and while he managed to support himself, he was known to borrow money from time to time to keep the water and electricity running in his house. On the night of Carl's death, neighbours witnessed another local resident, Colin Swayne, visiting his house, shouting and banging was heard but the police were only informed of his death some days later. Swayne was questioned about his involvement and some have speculated that Carl had owed money to Swayne who then confronted him, leading to his death.

Swayne, also known to the police as a drug user with a history of burglary and theft, was detained but then released after questioning and others have suggested that more people might have been involved in Carl's death. Despite his back injury, Carl was a tall and strong man, capable of defending himself in a fight, and many questioned whether Swayne could have overpowered him.

One other theory is that Swayne's home was known as a drug house, where county lines dealers would sometimes operate from. It is speculated that Carl's death may have been associated with the Boresfield line as a drug deal gone wrong, or even that Carl had used his old contacts to set up as a dealer himself. The investigation into his death is still ongoing, and while Swayne remains a primary suspect, police have yet to officially charge anyone with Porterhouse's murder.

It is the final unsolved case that has received the most media attention out of all, and has provoked a major review of the Hollenbourne police force. The recent killing of Sara Butt, a 24 year old dental student living with her parents on the Boresfield estate has shocked not only the local residents but people all over the country. After a high profile missing persons campaign and constant news coverage across all national broadcasters, Sara's body was found in the waters of Hollen marsh with forensic reports suggesting, initially, that she was bludgeoned to death with a blunt object. Later reports have suggested that the real cause of her death was asphyxiation through strangulation. Sara had lived with her British-Pakistani family in Hollenbourne all her life, attending Hollenbourne Comprehensive till the age of 18. She excelled in sciences and went on to study at Queen Mary University of London to become a dentist. Since 2020 her parents had had health problems and she decided to move to part time study, getting a job in a hair salon in the Piazza to supplement the family's income. When she wasn't at work or studying she was caring for her parents. Occasionally she would go out to see friends, taking the bus from Hollenbourne to the station and meeting them in Stratford or Thurrock. Her friends said that she was a pillar of their friend group, a kind and loyal person who was always willing to organise their get togethers and would reach out if she felt someone needed support. It was returning from a day out in Stratford to visit the Westfield shopping centre with her friends that she went missing on the 22nd of May. At 10:49pm she sent her last text to her parents — "back in 10" — after she got off the bus by the marshes on the edge of the Boresfield estate. Her parents' house was a 10 to 15 minute walk from this stop and between sending that text and heading home she was abducted, dragged into the marshland and then killed.

6 months on, this case appears to have yielded very few leads and pressure is once more on the Hollenbourne police force to find the killer.

Anger is growing amongst residents and trust in the police is at an all time low. For those living in the Boresfield estate, in particular, the recent history is a message to them that their lives and well-being do not matter. It is hard to say whether these 4 murders are all connected. Certainly, there are similarities in the ways in which the victims died and the area in which they resided and were sometimes found. What scares the residents of Boresfield increasingly is that there is a killer in their midst, hiding in plain sight, but invisible to the police whose concerns with the drug related violence have taken their attention away from what could be described as a pattern of violent offending against vulnerable people from within their community.

Hollenbourne has become a byline for violent county-lines violence, set in a bleak and deprived post-industrial landscape. But perhaps this image of 'Horrorbourne' has obscured a deeper, more primal, more nihilistic, form of violence that, to this day, afflicts its residents.
