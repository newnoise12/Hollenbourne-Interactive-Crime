# The Hollenbourne Case — Master Case Bible

*Ground truth and reference document. This is the staff-eyes version — what actually happened, in full. Student-facing material (evidence releases, redactions, weekly unlocks) is derived from this but reveals it partially and out of order. Two elements below are proposals, not decisions — marked clearly — everything else reflects what's already been agreed across the design conversation.*

**Setting note (confirmed):** Hollenbourne is anchored near Thurrock/Grays, South Essex, Thames-side — matching the town's own "industrial past serving London's dockyards" origin story with a real area that has genuine riverside-industrial and quarrying history. The A13 replaces the old A2/A203 as the arterial road and county lines corridor (a real, documented county lines route in this part of Essex). Real neighbouring towns for orientation: **Grays** and **Basildon** (working/post-industrial texture), **Brentwood** (affluent contrast point, in the old Bexleyheath role). Burgess lives in **Grays** — deliberately ordinary, not Hollenbourne itself, consistent with him being an outsider passing through for work rather than a local. Full town and estate geography now fixed below — any remaining Kent-era place references (Dartford/Swanley/Bexleyheath, A2/A203) should be checked against it. A visual map was attempted and set aside as not fit for purpose; this written geography is the authoritative reference.

**Town geography (confirmed):** The Thames runs along the southern edge of town. The A13 runs roughly east–west through the north of the town, connecting west toward Grays. **Critchley** sits north-west, near the A13 — the old cement works and its post-war tenement towers (including the Howill tower), older and more deprived. **North-Hollenbourne** sits north, directly above Featherton — Victorian terraces, medium-income, a distinct social character from both Critchley/Boresfield's post-war deprivation and Featherton's new-build aspiration; its central road, **Clements Road**, has a few shops, and it connects to Hollen Marsh's northern entrance. **Paget Street** is a residential street here — the doorbell camera location on Burgess's Butt-night route, part of the deliberately roundabout path via the high street that avoids Featherton's denser camera network. **Featherton** sits north-east, below North-Hollenbourne — newer, more aspirational development, commercial centre known as "the piazza." **Nigel Wooley lives in Featherton**, which is why his route to the marsh's northern entrance is a short, unremarkable walk from home, in contrast to Burgess's longer, deliberate detour around Featherton's camera network to reach the same entrance from outside. Hollenbourne high street and the railway station sit centrally. **Boresfield** sits south, older and more deprived, bordering Hollen Marsh directly. **Hollen Marsh** runs along the eastern side of town with two entrances: the northern one (reached via North-Hollenbourne/behind Featherton) and the southern one (the main entrance, via Boresfield — where the car park and Sara Butt's bus stop both sit).

**Boresfield estate geography (confirmed):** **Chertsey Street** is the main road running into the estate from the high street. **Harrison Road** — Susan Wooley's house, and Colin Swayne's flat; the **Londis** shop sits on its corner with Chertsey Street. **Bamford Street** — Carl Porterhouse's house; the **Robin Hood** pub sits on its corner near Carpenter Street. **Folkestone Road** — Holly Creagan's house (near the marsh), Sara Butt's parents' house, and Geoff Mason's house, the southernmost on the road, backing directly onto the marsh. **Blenheim Street** and **Carpenter Street** are cross streets running through the estate. **Marsh Road** runs adjacent to the marsh itself, past Colin Swayne's flat down to the car park and southern marsh entrance — this is where Khalid Haddad makes his stop for an old contact on the Wooley evening (see Haddad's entry, Appendix I).

**Critchley Plant (confirmed):** a cement works, not steel — matching the real, dominant historical industry of this exact stretch of the Thames (Grays/West Thurrock), where cement manufacturing defined the local economy from the mid-19th century. Employed roughly **3,000 workers at its height** — an integrated operation spanning the chalk quarry, kilns, works, and its own rail/barge network, plausibly peaking in the 1930s–1950s before serious automation set in — dwindling to a much smaller crew by closure, reflecting a generation of quiet decline rather than a sudden collapse. Geoff Mason started at the plant in 1954; **it closed in 1982**, giving him 28 years there. Reasons worth reusing directly: chalk reserves ran out over time, partly because the town's own growth had encroached on the quarry and cut off further expansion; cheaper foreign imports undercut UK producers through the '70s; and the plant finally closed amid the severe early-1980s UK recession that ended a huge swathe of British heavy industry — a broadly recognisable real anchor for a general podcast audience, arguably stronger than a narrower single-industry date. The industry didn't just decline, it was slowly strangled by the same town it built. The plant's tall chimneys (needed to increase kiln draught) are the real-world basis for "the long shadow of the chimneys falling over Boresfield" already in Hollenbourne's founding description.

Butt's death is the one that sparks renewed public and media attention — not Porterhouse's, despite Porterhouse being more recent in earlier drafts of this timeline. A rising background rate of violent crime plus a death methodologically similar to Mason's and Wooley's is what forces Essex police to publicly concede a possible link, which is when "serial killer" enters the coverage.

## Victim biographies

*From the podcast script — confirmed canon. Kept compact here; full prose lives in the source prose archive.*

**Geoff Mason.** Born working-class in Deptford, lived through the Blitz. Moved to Hollenbourne in 1954 for work at the new Critchley cement plant; met his wife Doreen that same year, married 1957. Lived first in the Howill tower on the Critchley estate, then bought in Boresfield once Doreen was expecting their first child (1958) — three children raised there. Geoff never liked the marsh himself; Doreen did. Worked at Critchley for the plant's last 28 years before its 1982 closure, which he felt as a real, ongoing grief. Retired at 60 after a spell as a gardener. Doreen was diagnosed with dementia in 2014, died 2017 — after which Geoff took up walking the marsh himself, specifically because it made him feel closer to her. That walking habit is what puts him there regularly enough for Burgess's account of him to be credible, and is the emotional reason he's on the marsh at all in the years before his death.

**Susan Wooley.** Grew up in Tottenham, a bright, ambitious child of a Caribbean family. Studied Chemistry at Reading (from 1982), trained as a teacher, taught in northeast London secondary schools — well-regarded, former students speak warmly of her. First marriage to Daniel Williams (a hardware shop owner), 1991, Walthamstow; separated 2010. Moved to Hollenbourne alone afterward, bought her own house, took the teaching assistant post at **St Martin's primary school**, got involved in church and food-bank volunteering. Met Nigel in 2012 through this new Hollenbourne life, not before it. Full relationship arc with Nigel already established elsewhere in this document — this fills in who she was independently of him.

**Sara Butt.** British-Pakistani family, lived in Hollenbourne her entire life — the only one of the four victims who is a genuine lifelong local rather than someone who arrived as an adult. Attended Hollenbourne Comprehensive to 18, excelled in sciences, went on to study dentistry at **Queen Mary University of London** (the real East London dental school — its outreach clinics run in Canary Wharf, Stratford, and Homerton, which gives her Stratford social trips a plausible secondary reason too). Moved to part-time study from 2020 once her parents' health began to decline, taking a job at a hair salon in the Piazza to help support the family alongside caring for them directly. Well-liked, described by friends as the one who held the friend group together. Killed returning from a day out with friends in Stratford (Westfield). Note the podcast's own framing: it's recorded roughly six months after her death, which is the in-universe reason relatively little is confirmed about her compared to Mason or Wooley — this is presented as an active, ongoing case, not yet a settled retrospective.

**Carl Porterhouse** — deliberately kept thin in the podcast (tied to his not being an "ideal victim," per Christie), and thin here too by design. A few confirmed added details: known to drink at the Robin Hood pub, well-liked there despite his record; a back injury from labouring work ended his ability to do manual work and contributed to his declining health in the years before his death; borrowed money periodically to keep his utilities running, consistent with his established financial precarity. No fuller biography intended — see the Colin Swayne / Porterhouse sections below for the actual events of his death.

## The true solution

### Burgess killed Mason, Wooley, and Butt

**Access and method (MO):** Burgess is a gas and boiler technician with legitimate, unremarkable reasons to be in and around people's homes. He serviced Mason's boiler prior to Mason's death and was in the area servicing a neighbour of Wooley's on the day she died — both genuine customer relationships, not fabricated alibis. For Mason and Wooley, his method is trusted-tradesman access: he is let in, or is a familiar enough presence nearby that nobody thinks twice. For Butt, his method changes — he abducts her near, rather than entering a property — a real escalation in risk-tolerance and confidence.

**Psychology (signature) — confirmed, kept backgrounded.** Burgess's underlying driver is real and specific, but it is *ground truth for design purposes only* — not something the game foregrounds or hands students directly. It sits underneath the case as texture and internal consistency, not as a revealed thesis.

Burgess grew up poor, with an abusive father and childhood marked by witnessed domestic violence. He left home as early as he could, worked through trade apprenticeships into boiler engineering. Withdrawn at school, with a history of adolescent delinquency and an occasional capacity for extreme, tightly-regulated violence that periodically broke through. In early adulthood he was tried for manslaughter — a nightclub altercation, no direct CCTV of the incident — and the case collapsed for insufficient evidence. This is left deliberately ambiguous: it is not established whether Mason was Burgess's first killing, or whether the manslaughter charge concealed an earlier one. A handful of short adult relationships ended in arguments serious enough that police were called; no charges ever followed. He now lives alone in Grays, with few friends — outwardly unremarkable.

The trigger with Mason: Burgess's father had recently died, leaving unresolved feelings. Something in the encounter with Mason evokes the father, and the response is close to instinctive — displaced rage at a symbolic stand-in, not a planned killing. His clothing (work boots, jeans, dark puffer jacket, hood) across every sighting regardless of season is simply what he wears for work — unremarkable in almost any context, and not necessarily a deliberate disguise.

**The Mason killing (8 October 2019) — the first, and the messiest.** Burgess is at Mason's home for a scheduled boiler service. Something Mason does or says triggers the displaced response; Burgess pushes him, strikes him in the mouth, then — anxious about his job and still aroused/angry — takes a wrench and strikes him four or five more times to the head and neck. This is improvised, not planned; he is already wearing work gloves. He fetches a tarpaulin from his van, wraps the body, drags it out the back door (around 3:45pm, dusk approaching but not yet dark), and carries it — to any onlooker, a tradesman moving building materials — to a secluded spot in the marshes. He returns, locks the back door, and shakily finishes the boiler service, backdating the paperwork to the time he would have finished had nothing happened. He leaves by the front door with a deliberately ordinary parting line — "goodbye, take care" — and drives home in his work van.

Only on the drive home does he register the tarpaulin as a liability. He calls his mother to borrow his late father's car — a black 4x4, registration not yet transferred following the father's recent death, which is precisely why a hypothetical vehicle check would be unlikely to connect it to Burgess. He waits for full dark and reaches Hollen Marsh car park around 9:30pm, retrieves the tarpaulin, then realises he's leaving a second, distinct set of tracks in the soft ground — so he leaves by a different route, on foot, through a secluded residential street, back to the car. *(This street is a live evidence opportunity not yet built into the game — a doorbell camera or dashcam catching an unremarkable man on foot, late, going nowhere he obviously belongs.)* He drops the car back at his mother's and walks home.

His emotional aftermath is not what he expects of himself: no horror, no self-disgust, no guilt — that capacity closes off in this moment. Alongside anxiety about being caught sits a quiet satisfaction — he has given his father, via Mason's symbolic stand-in, what he believes he deserved — and a sense of power he associates with no longer being the frightened child he once was.

**The interview (16 October 2019) — the closest he ever comes to being caught.** As one of the last people to see Mason alive, Burgess is questioned by Hollenbourne police. He's cautioned but declines a solicitor, working on the theory that talking freely lets him find out what the police actually have and steer around it. He's frightened — genuinely aware of what 25 years inside would mean — but also strangely detached, as though watching himself as the protagonist of an unfolding story rather than living it. That detachment is what lets him perform calm: affable, concerned, proactively "helpful." He claims he only learned of the death from the local paper days later and had been planning to come forward anyway, since he'd been at the property hours before Mason died.

He gives a detailed, accurate account of the visit — arrival, the conversation with Mason, the routine service, departure at 4pm — opening with some general, rapport-building questions about his work, and a direct challenge over his manslaughter charge, which he deflects with mild, believable discomfort rather than real strain. The officer eases off rather than pressing further, moving to something more neutral — where he lives — before returning to the substance of the day. The police push back on the timeline: his work van's telematics show it didn't actually leave the area until 4:45. Burgess covers the 45-minute gap with two claims, improvised in the moment: that he'd worked through lunch and stopped nearby afterwards for a sandwich and the radio (roughly 30 minutes), and — the sharper improvisation — that the boiler service itself finished at 3:45 as logged, but he'd stayed on a few minutes afterward because Mason seemed anxious about something, though all Mason would say was that "the neighbourhood wasn't what it used to be." Between the two claims, his account stretches to roughly 4:10–4:40 — close enough to the real 4:45 that it holds. His phone data places him in the immediate area for that hour, which reads as corroboration — but only corroborates *where* he was, not *what he was doing there*. Forensics and testimony appear to line up. Police remain quietly suspicious, but nothing solidifies.

**What was never asked, and is still there to find.** Nobody thought to ask which tool Burgess used to open and service the boiler, or requested a search of his van and toolkit. If they had, they'd have found a spanner conspicuously cleaner than the rest of his set — and matching the indentations in Geoff Mason's skull. This is a live, physical, discoverable piece of evidence that was simply never chased in 2019. It's arguably the single strongest "if only" in the whole case, and — unlike the vehicle-registration gap — it's concrete enough to become an actual student-facing lead: something reachable only by the right chain of investigative reasoning (matching the wound pattern to a tool type, then requesting a search of a suspect's toolkit), which makes it a genuinely satisfying discovery to design toward rather than a fact to simply hand over. Not yet built into the evidence board or action economy — worth flagging as a priority once mechanics resume.

**The tell, and the misdirection.** At one point in the interview Burgess refers to "Geoff" as "Rich" — his own father's name — a single unconscious slip, corrected in the same breath, passed over without comment by either officer. It's genuinely minor: a moment a very close reading might catch, not one the interview itself dwells on. Separately, right at the close of the interview, directly challenged on whether he was involved, Burgess reaches for county lines as an explanation — the only point in the interview where it comes up:

> **Interviewer:** Mr Burgess, did you have any involvement in the death of Geoff Mason?
> **Burgess:** No. Absolutely not, no.
> **Interviewer:** Do you have any idea who did it?
> **Burgess:** No, I don't. All I can think is that it's one of those estates, you know? Drugs and that. Kids...
> **Interviewer:** Could you be more specific?
> **Burgess:** I mean you're always hearing about Hollenbourne and county lines stuff. People kicking people out of their houses and selling drugs from there. You know more about it than me.
> **Interviewer:** Did you see anything that made you think that Geoff was involved in that?
> **Burgess:** No, not really, but like I said he seemed stressed about something. I wondered if it was that.

This changes what the county-lines misdirection actually *is* in this case. It isn't only institutional bias sitting around waiting to be picked up — Burgess correctly judges that it's the story Hollenbourne's reputation has already primed police to want, and in this one moment, under direct pressure, he hands it to them. (Full transcript in Appendix A, close to verbatim reuse as a student-facing exhibit. **Resolved: stays as dramatic irony and contextual justification — not built into a discoverable action.** It explains why the original 2019 investigation had a plausible, non-negligent reason to look elsewhere; it isn't meant to be a clue for students to find and act on.)

**The case goes cold.** Forensic evidence on Mason's body is limited by prolonged outdoor exposure; Burgess's DNA at the scene is fully explained by his legitimate boiler visit. One finding does survive, though, and sits in the original 2019 report unremarked: a small quantity of synthetic fibre, inconsistent with Mason's own clothing, recovered from the margins of the skull fractures — protected from the elements by being embedded in the wound and dried blood rather than sitting loose on the body. Noted as insufficient for origin determination at the time, not pursued further. This is real, findable evidence that the body was wrapped for transport (the tarpaulin) — and it directly answers a question the original file left open (killed on the marsh, or killed elsewhere and moved) for anyone who reads the report closely enough to connect it. Baseline information, not gated behind a costed action — a competent 2019 examination would have recorded this regardless of anyone's investigative choices; the point is that nobody read it as significant, not that it was hard to find. Neighbours have little to say about the quiet widower who sometimes walked the marshland. Local residents confirm county lines activity in the area generally, but nobody connects Mason to it specifically. Police remain quietly suspicious of Burgess without anything to act on.

**2019–2022: the sense of impunity grows.** Burgess's life resumes. He hears nothing further. Talking to police without being caught convinces him they have nothing — and the sense of having got away with it curdles into something closer to grandiosity: the feeling of being the protagonist of his own unfolding story, smarter than everyone around him, perhaps the only genuinely intelligent person in the world.

COVID lockdown (2020) accelerates this. Furloughed for a stretch, almost totally isolated (contact narrowed to his mother and occasionally his sister), he cycles through rumination and outbursts — on one occasion putting in a call serious enough that police conduct a welfare check, find him agitated but not a danger, note a claimed dropped plate and rearranged furniture, and close it with no further action. *(This is a real, logged, findable police record — worth keeping in mind as a future documentary exhibit.)* Through this period he rewrites his own history in his head — the wrongs of his childhood, his adolescence, his failed relationships — building a private case for his own restored dignity.

By mid-2021, easing restrictions and a severe backlog of trade demand pull him back into regular work — plausible timing; heating engineers were broadly classed as essential throughout, so full furlough for a year is a slightly generous read, but partial/flexible furlough through reduced-demand stretches fits comfortably, and the pent-up post-lockdown trade backlog he returns into was real and well documented. His company's mask policy suits him — cover for saying little, showing less. Where he'd once had easy patter with customers, he now does the job and leaves.

By late 2021 he's in Hollenbourne almost weekly, working poorly-maintained rentals for landlords slow to replace failing boilers. His attitude toward the area has curdled too: where he was once sympathetic to residents left in substandard housing, he now blames the community, the police, and the local authority — in a displaced, backwards logic — for failing to protect Mason, or catch his killer.

### The Wooley killing (6 January 2022)

Burgess's booked job that day is next door — a documented, invoiced call-out to a neighbour, timed for mid-afternoon. Wooley catches him on his way back to the van around 3:30pm, or the neighbour mentions in passing that Susan's boiler has been temperamental too, and she asks, since he's already there, whether he'd mind taking a quick look. It's the kind of small, informal favour tradespeople get asked constantly — nothing booked, nothing logged, no reason for it to exist on paper at all. There's something in that off-the-books quality — unplanned, improvised, off-script — that faintly echoes the first killing's own improvisation, and it may be exactly that quality that starts to stir something in him before he's even through her door.

Her house breaks the pattern he's come to expect of Hollenbourne: a kept lawn, tidy beds, no leftover Christmas decorations. Her boiler won't hold pressure. It's a January afternoon already sliding toward dusk, and despite a cold house — no heating, no daylight left to speak of — she's warm herself: makes him tea, asks about his Christmas, draws him into conversation that comes stiffly at first, like a muscle unused for a year. It is, in some sense, the first real human contact he's had in a year.

**Worth flagging as a deliberate design choice rather than a gap:** unlike Mason, there's no clear resemblance-trigger here. By your own account, Burgess "couldn't himself tell you why" — thirty-five minutes after arriving, around 4:15pm, Wooley is dead on her kitchen floor. Read alongside the ad hoc, unlogged nature of the visit itself, this reads as a real and coherent escalation: the first kill emotionally legible, however dark, and tied to a specific resemblance; the second closer to autonomous, triggered as much by circumstance — an improvised, unrecorded visit — as by anything Wooley herself did.

This time he leaves no paperwork, and undoes any visible sign the boiler was touched — a deliberate absence of a record, unlike Mason. Consistent with the established file: no signs of forced entry, because she let in someone she had informal, in-the-moment reason to trust. He says goodbye to a second dead body on his way out, and drives on, in something like a daze, to his next appointment — a boiler in the newly built Featherton estate, opened and serviced with the same tool, now carrying transferred trace of Wooley's blood. *(Confirmed as intentional literary flourish, not a findable clue — the trace exists, and is never recovered by anyone, in or out of the story.)*

**Why Burgess evades real scrutiny here.** Three things hold, none of them requiring investigators to be careless:

1. **Nigel Wooley absorbs the investigation's attention, legitimately.** Motive, history, a restraining-order breach placing him physically nearby, DNA at the scene, an evasive account — any real investigation prioritises that suspect over a long list of "was in the area" names, no authorial thumb on the scale required.
2. **No formal record ties Burgess to Wooley's address at all** — only to the neighbour's, which is unremarkable and easily lost in a routine canvass. There was never a booking, invoice, or call log with her name on it.
3. **Nobody cross-references back to Mason.** Burgess was formally interviewed in 2019, but that file is closed and three years old; nothing about a routine 2022 door-to-door would prompt anyone to check his name against an unrelated, unsolved case in a different year. That kind of cross-case matching only happens once investigators suspect a *series* — which doesn't happen until Butt's death years later. This is the real engine of the whole premise: the connections were sitting in two separate, closed files the whole time, genuinely unreachable to anyone without a reason to compare them — which is exactly the job your review panel exists to do.

**The vehicle, summarised for the institutional-failure thread:** the dark 4x4 is the late father's car, borrowed via the mother, registration not yet updated. Nobody cross-referenced it at the time, and the honest reason is institutional, not incompetent: Mason's case, an isolated elderly man with no immediate suspect, never generated enough investigative weight in 2019 to justify a DVLA check on vehicles near the scene. The dark figure of crime made concrete — the underinvestment happened at the very first opportunity, not later.

**On the DNA — confirmed, and better than the earlier version of this explanation.** A speculative database search against DNA recovered from Wooley's scene *would* have been run routinely — this isn't a case of it never being checked. It comes back clean because Burgess's profile almost certainly wasn't on the National DNA Database by 2022 at all, for two independent, real reasons. First: his manslaughter case was never a conviction, and under the Protection of Freedoms Act 2012, profiles from an arrest/charge that doesn't lead to conviction can only be retained for a capped period — long since lapsed by 2022 given "early adulthood" puts that case well over a decade prior. Second, and more concretely: the 2012 Act triggered a mass deletion exercise, completed around 2013, removing hundreds of thousands of legacy profiles from people arrested but never convicted — almost exactly the right moment to have caught and deleted his, nine years before Wooley's death, with nobody needing to make an active decision about him at all. Separately, his 2019 Mason sample would have been given as an elimination sample (a known, legitimate visitor, not a suspect), and elimination samples are generally destroyed once no longer needed for that specific investigation — which a cold, unsolved case would have triggered in due course.

The honest version, then: the check was done correctly, and correctly found nothing. Not a gap in police diligence, but a lawful, privacy-protecting outcome — the same 2012 reform that exists to stop innocent people being kept on a genetic database indefinitely, quietly working in a guilty man's favour. Genuinely good material for the moral-reasoning strand of the module: individual privacy rights versus detection efficacy, playing out with real stakes rather than as an abstract debate.

### Aftermath of Wooley, and the road to Butt (2022–2025)

The Wooley killing affects Burgess differently to Mason's. He gets through the rest of the day and collapses on getting home. Both killings were unplanned, but this one feels entirely outside his control — he has no memory of the act itself, only surfacing again at his next appointment to find his spanner slick with drying blood, nearly vomiting into his mask from the stress. *(If interviewed, the homeowners at that next appointment would describe him as sweating, short-tempered, hurried — and would recall him abruptly moving his toolbox out of sight before an awkward explanation about a valve. Nobody was ever interviewed. This is a real, buried lead — now formalised as a proper action, see Open Items.)*

He doesn't feel guilt exactly, but a sense of having made a mistake — practical, and perhaps moral. He follows the case obsessively: local news, Facebook discussion groups, whatever he can piece together about Wooley's life online. He knows the date of her funeral. This is a wake-up call, not a moral one — a recognition that his unravelling mental state has stopped being the thing that let him feel powerful and started being a liability. Professional help isn't a real option — the fear isn't therapy itself, but what might come out of it, and what a formal record might trigger.

His mother is diagnosed with a terminal illness not long after. As the sibling living closest, he takes on her care — daily visits, staying over in her final months, hospital appointments — through to her death in March 2023. Grief brings an unexpected sense of closure with both parents now gone, and a period of real reconnection with his sister, now able to speak more openly about their father as adults. The two years that follow are genuinely stable: old friendships resume, and in late 2024 he begins a relationship with a woman met through a dating app. His self-justification hardens during this period rather than fading — a conviction that the world has tacitly forgiven him by simply letting him get away with it, that Hollenbourne is worthless, that the people he killed would likely have died some other violent or self-destructive way regardless.

The relationship ends in early 2025, two months after she moves into his flat — she would describe him, if asked, as detached, avoidant, working long hours to be away from her, prone to sudden anger over small things, and frightening in a way she could never quite name as violence. The breakup reopens the old pattern. There's no work booked in Hollenbourne, but he finds himself heading back anyway — on 22 May, taking a bus to the Critchley estate and walking the back streets, through Featherton, into the marshes where Mason's body was left years before. He doesn't know if he intends to kill. He knows he wants to feel scornful, muttering contempt under his breath at people he passes. He settles near where Mason was found, watching a bus stop, waiting — in his own account of himself — for something else to make the decision for him.

### The Butt killing (22 May 2025)

It arrives at 10:49. Sara Butt gets off the bus alone — no connecting service from that stop, and a walk of ten to fifteen minutes back to her parents' house on Boresfield. The first stretch, past the marsh, is unlit and empty; only further along does it give way to the lit, residential streets. She pauses to send a last WhatsApp message to her parents — *"back in 10"* — a real, recoverable timestamp, and a genuine estimate of the walk ahead of her. He crosses to her once she's over the road, while she's still on the isolated stretch. He has to move fast: he runs at her, she manages one cut-off sound before his hand closes over her jaw, and he drags her back across the road into the marsh.

For the first time, there's no tool — this wasn't planned as a kill, so nothing was brought to make one easy. He strangles her by hand. Unlike Mason and Wooley, there is no gap in his memory afterward — he is present for the whole of it, in a way he wasn't for the others. Had Mason still been alive, his house — the nearest to the marsh — might have put him in earshot of her cut-off scream; Butt herself is too far from the residential streets for anyone to hear.

Rather than leaving her where Mason was left, Burgess carries her to the wetter part of the marsh and works her body under roughly two inches of water — soaking his trousers and shoes to the knee in the process. She's found eight days later by a dog walker who spots her red shirt — long enough that a missing-persons appeal and media campaign is already running by the time she's discovered, having been reported missing within a day or so of not returning home. The discovery lands as the grim answer to a search the public was already emotionally invested in, rather than a cold find.

**On cause of death — proposed resolution to a real continuity point:** the original case record describes Butt as bludgeoned, matching Mason and Wooley. Given the account above, the true mechanism is manual strangulation. Rather than contradicting the record, this is best read as the record being wrong in exactly the way the whole case is about: five days partly submerged, with bruising and trauma from the struggle and disposal, plausibly reads as consistent with a beating on initial examination — which is what locks in the "same killer, same method" narrative. The true cause, apparent only on a proper post-mortem, is strangulation. This isn't a patch, it's another version of the case's central point: the obvious, pattern-matching reading is wrong, and nobody who mattered looked closely enough to catch it before the story had already hardened. It also explains the MO change cleanly — he has no tool because nothing about this was planned as a kill.

### Porterhouse — the county lines death

**Confirmed (supersedes the earlier proposed version above).** Porterhouse's death is not part of Burgess's pattern — it's a genuine county lines killing, swept into the "serial killer" narrative by superficial similarity (bludgeoning) rather than real connection. The actual mechanism is not a calculated hit but an unplanned escalation:

**The gang dynamic.** A county line first identifies in the area in 2017 — a North West London operation, leadership currently attributed to someone known as **Madz** — plausibly the older, more established presence around Critchley and its tenements. In 2019 a second, less-understood operation opens a line specifically targeting Boresfield, linked to an East London outfit; a mid-level operative known as **Shacks** is the closest identifiable link. The 2019 territorial collision between the two gives Hollenbourne's official "rising violence tied to county lines gangs" explanation a real cause, not just an assertion — and gives Mason's unrelated 2019 death useful, genuine noise to hide inside.

**Shacks stays deliberately shadowy — no full biography, no surname, referenced only secondhand.** This is a considered choice, not a gap: real county lines hierarchies are rarely traced to the top even after successful local prosecutions, and keeping him unresolved is more honest to that than naming him would be. It also means Burgess's thread can resolve completely and satisfyingly while the county lines thread stays properly murky — institutional failure that doesn't tidy into an answer, same as it often doesn't in reality. **The "don't touch — we're building a case on this guy" line attaches to Shacks specifically** — plausibly under surveillance by a regional organised crime unit or the NCA (given any real case against him would span force boundaries into East London) as part of a larger operation, not for anything Porterhouse-related. Local disruption over a minor, Porterhouse-adjacent matter would burn that bigger case — a real trade-off with a real cost, not a plot convenience.

**The killing itself.** Porterhouse is a user known to Colin — not a childhood friend, but someone who occupies similar circles, arrived in Hollenbourne in 2010, well known to police and users alike. In care for much of his childhood, came to drugs later than Colin; ten years in prison for armed robbery and aggravated assault, part of an organised crime outfit operating across East London until the early 2000s; four years into a growing crack addiction by the time of his death, but carries himself differently to most users — Colin has real admiration for him, sees him as someone who can handle himself.

One evening Porterhouse calls Colin's flat wanting a £40 bag; Colin, assuming he'll be offered a share, brings it over himself rather than have Porterhouse collect it, telling the two dealers occupying his property — **Kato** and **Reece** — that he'll bring the money back. They agree. At Porterhouse's, the two smoke together; after forty minutes the dealers call, accusing Colin of stealing the drugs. Colin tries to placate them. Porterhouse, hearing Colin's demure, apologetic tone, takes it as a slight against his own character — shouts, loud enough for the dealers to hear down the phone, that they should "fucking come and get it themselves." They hang up without another word. Porterhouse, who genuinely hates the predatory nature of the county lines arrangement, tells Colin to "get rid" of them.

Five minutes later, a knock. Porterhouse answers; Kato and Reece push their way in and start on him immediately. Colin — allegiance to Porterhouse pulling one way, his whole entangled life pulling the other — stands aside while Porterhouse is punched and kicked. A heavy ornament is taken from a shelf and used to knock him unconscious with a single brutal blow to the back of the head. Colin is hit around the face, but the three quickly realise Porterhouse isn't responding, and try to cover their tracks — the ornament removed for disposal, the drugs taken, all three returning to Colin's flat on the next street. Business ends early for the night. It isn't clear, even to Kato and Reece, that they've killed him — his body isn't found for several days. Colin is left alone, no longer waiting for his own death, but actively inviting it.

**Colin and Mason — a live open thread, not yet resolved.** Worth building deliberate ambiguity here: Mason's death has no obvious county lines evidence connecting it to anything, but there's a real school of thought within Hollenbourne Police that most local violence traces back to the line in some way — three of the four deaths (Mason, Wooley, Butt) sit outside that explanation, which is itself a pattern worth Ferris (or the panel) noticing. Two ideas to build toward, not yet written:
- Mason may have been previously bothered by dealers — approached about using his shed, minor intimidation, possibly reported and nothing came of it — genuine county-lines-adjacent noise around him without it being *true*, exactly the kind of thing that would let Burgess's own misdirection in his 2019 interview land as convincingly as it does.
- Colin may reveal, if re-interviewed, that he knew Mason — a real, findable detail, but only reachable through a specific action (re-interviewing Swayne), not handed over. This should be built as a reward for that specific investigative choice, consistent with how the rest of the action economy already works.

This gives Porterhouse's thread its own version of the same institutional failure the Burgess thread makes elsewhere: the force settles on the obvious local suspect — known to them, previous convictions, visible poverty and drug use — rather than doing the harder work of identifying who was actually giving the orders. Colin is genuinely entangled in county lines, genuinely evasive, and genuinely not the one who killed his friend.

### Colin Swayne — biography

Lived in and around Hollenbourne all his life. Growing up low-income near Critchley Plant meant early exposure to more than most children his age. His father died by suicide when Colin was 9, leaving his mother with three boys; she held things together with her own parents' help, but as the boys grew older and processed their father's death differently, she began to withdraw into long bouts of depression herself. Colin's own decline starts young and escalates steadily: drunk for the first time at 11, cannabis at 12, regularly using stimulants with friends who roam the marshland around Boresfield by night at 14. He stops attending school regularly and leaves at 15 with no qualifications, doing casual work with local bands — loading vans, travelling to gigs — poorly paid but giving him easy access to drugs. He has no drug of choice, only the need to feel outside himself for a while. Nights away from home stretch into days, then longer; the pretence of roadie work gives way to petty theft and burglary of industrial sites to fund a now well-established addiction. He lives in squats in Dagenham and Romford.

**2004** — arrested and given a 4-year sentence for a string of offences. **2006** — released, returns to live with his mother, still using; quickly thrown out with his older brother's encouragement. Homeless in Dagenham for a year. **2008** — arrested again after burglarising a house, given a 7-year sentence (plausibly his third domestic burglary conviction, which under real sentencing law carries a statutory minimum of 3 years, with his record and the professional nature of the offence pushing it well beyond that). During this sentence he borrows to buy spice and mamba, then just reaching the prison estate — accurately timed to the real 2013–14 UK prison synthetic-drugs problem — and is severely beaten over unpaid debts, suffering a concussion. From this point he experiences memory loss, confusion, and anger, later diagnosed — after a probation-mandated substance misuse course on his 2014 release — with an **Acquired Brain Injury**. He begins receiving PIP (correctly the current disability benefit for a 2014 diagnosis, having replaced DLA for working-age claimants the year before).

He lives with his younger brother for a year on the condition he stays sober. In 2015 his mother is diagnosed terminally ill and he moves into temporary accommodation in Hollenbourne to care for her — two relapses follow, only one ever known to his brother, so the family narrative that holds is "sober, sorted to some extent, just flaky and unreliable because of his head injury." He does his best for her, though professionals carry most of the actual care. She dies in 2018; what had been occasional relapses gives way to full addiction again. Her flat is sold; his own social housing application is finally granted in 2019, and he moves into a flat on Boresfield. A new county line is opening up to target the estate. Now around 40, struggling to feed his addiction on disability benefits, Colin agrees to let his property be used to sell drugs, in exchange for money and drugs of his own — the arrangement that becomes his cuckooing.

**Continues into:** the Porterhouse killing, written in full above — Colin present throughout, not the one who struck the fatal blow.

### The other two suspects

**Nigel Wooley** — real motive, real history, real evasiveness, and entirely innocent of the murder. His DNA at the scene is real but undateable (he cohabited with Susan until 2021); his presence near Hollen Marsh with the dog is real and unconnected to her death. He's the suspect the investigation is *most* motivated to pursue and least able to actually convict, which is the point — an obvious, sympathetic-to-suspect narrative that happens to be wrong. **By 2025:** semi-retired, occasionally consulted by former colleagues; two daughters he stays in touch with; lives in a Featherton flat with a concierge. Routine: dog walk around 8–8:30am to the marshes, coffee and croissant in the Piazza around 9:30–10am, adult learning (art history) at Dagenham College Mondays and Wednesdays, 12–3pm, shops at the Co-op in Featherton or the big Tesco by Critchley, second dog walk around 6pm — occasionally as far as the Red Lion (a pub on the far side of the marsh, distinct from the Robin Hood in Boresfield) before turning back. Dog's name: Missy (female).

**Khalid Haddad** — entirely innocent of all four deaths, but not a clean cipher: a real biography gives police (and students) a substantive, defensible reason to look at him beyond simple proximity, which is what makes the profiling critique land rather than feeling arbitrary.

Arrives in the UK in 2015, aged 15, with his mother and two younger sisters, fleeing Syria. The family's claim sees them dispersed to Rochdale; he gets a single disrupted year of schooling — the right to education isn't contingent on the claim being decided, but arriving mid-cycle at 15 with little English leaves him functionally literate and little more, formally. Status granted in 2017, the family moves to Dagenham for its existing community, and Haddad — now 17, with no practical route back into education — takes work as a kitchen porter.

Around 18, through that world, he's drawn into the fringes of a county lines operation opening a route through Hornchurch — holding phones, being somewhere for an hour, not asking questions. In late 2018 he's arrested in a police operation targeting the line, found in a property being used to store product alongside two older men who clearly are dealing. A sharp duty solicitor spots the exploitation pattern immediately — a teenager, no record, drawn in within two years of arriving as an asylum claimant — and pushes an NRM referral before charge. Months later the case is dropped: a Section 45 Modern Slavery Act defence, exploitation as direct cause of the offence. He's never convicted of anything.

It isn't the arrest that gets him out, though — it's his mother, in the days after. Her argument to him isn't abstract danger; it's that the family crossed a continent for exactly one reason — her children's safety — and everything since 2015 was in service of that, and he's put it at risk anyway, not just for himself but for his sisters, because these people now know his name, and knowing his name means knowing where they live. She's frightened and furious in equal measure. That's what actually reaches him, not the police. **Worth knowing: he would plausibly say something like this himself, defensively, if ever pushed in interview to explain why he "got away with it."**

By the time of the Hollenbourne deaths he's 26, settled, working as a delivery driver out of Ilford — a job whose entire shape is wide-ranging, irregular presence across a large patch, which is exactly what makes his phone and bike data light up near Mason, Porterhouse, and Butt's deaths across three unrelated years. For Mason and Butt specifically, this is genuinely occupational — no story beyond correlation, and no story needed. Porterhouse and Wooley are different: both have real, specific occasions behind them. Porterhouse — already established — is the cuckooed-flat visit, buying casually from whoever's around at a Boresfield flat connected to Swayne. Wooley is earlier and different in kind: January 2022 is barely a year after he walked away from the Hornchurch line, early enough that the pull hadn't fully faded and before his current stable delivery job existed. An old contact asks a small favour — a pickup on **Marsh Road**, dropped off shortly after in Featherton, folded invisibly into a route he's already driving — and he agrees, thinking little of it at the time. He knows neither the person who asked nor the person he hands it to; not evasion, just genuinely how these low-level favours work. Nothing relevant to Susan Wooley's death; he sees nothing, knows nothing, and wouldn't remember the evening at all if it weren't for this. **How this becomes visible: not found by the original 2022 investigation** (Nigel absorbed all the resourcing that year; nobody ran movement data beyond the doorstep-level canvass) **but by a 2025 review team who already knows about Haddad from Porterhouse, thinking to cross-reference his data against the other case timeframes.** An earned discovery, not a coincidence handed over — see the action economy reference for the costed action this requires.

**Retired:** an earlier idea had Haddad as a potential witness to the dark 4x4, given his delivery route. Superseded by the traffic camera action in the Mason movements chain, which is now the sole, mechanically real way to find the vehicle — kept as one clean path rather than two overlapping ones.

**Resolved: his NRM/2018 arrest record is a real, findable paper trail** — a formal PNC/CRIS and Home Office record, not a recollection, deliberately the opposite texture to Ferris's Burgess unease so the case doesn't lean on the same device twice. Full record in procedural documents; costed action in the action economy reference.

## Institutional failure map

These are the threads the institutional-review side of the game should keep surfacing. Not yet tied to specific weeks (the week-by-week structure is still being redrafted separately) — organised here by theme instead.

- **Mason (dark figure of crime):** an isolated elderly victim generates minimal investigative weight; no vehicle check ever requested; the case is effectively closed within a week of being opened. The clearest, earliest example of underinvestment, not incompetence.
- **Wooley (tunnel vision):** the investigation's energy goes almost entirely toward Nigel Wooley — a real suspect with real motive — at the expense of noticing Burgess's real, mundane, twice-documented presence in the area.
- **Porterhouse (convenient suspect):** county lines is correctly identified as the context, but Swayne — visible, known, poor, previously convicted — absorbs all the suspicion that should have gone toward whoever was actually running the operation above him.
- **Haddad (profiling as suspect vs. listening as witness):** the clearest structural point in the whole case — treating someone as a suspect because his movements fit a pattern means nobody asks him what he saw.
- **Butt ("ideal victim"):** why does *this* death trigger the public and institutional response the other three didn't? Worth teaching against Nils Christie's "ideal victim" concept directly — not that the earlier deaths mattered less, but that nobody with power was positioned to demand a response until now.
- **AI/documentary reliability** (placement still under review, per earlier discussion): if kept, the natural home is wherever AI-drafted reporting can be shown distorting or flattening a real account — e.g. a summarised version of a witness statement that smooths over the very uncertainty that would have mattered.

**HOLLENBOURNE POLICE**
**Internal memorandum**

To: Divisional Commander; Hollenbourne Case Review Panel
From: DS H. Ferris
Date: 2 June 2025
Re: Possible connections between recent deaths — Mason (2019), Wooley (2022), Porterhouse (2023), Butt (2025)

I've been asked to set out, informally and ahead of the panel's formal review, why I think these four deaths warrant being looked at together rather than as four separate matters.

### Sara Butt — follow-up report (reference HP-FS-2025-0203-S)

**HOLLENBOURNE POLICE FORENSIC SERVICES**
**Post-mortem examination report — SUPPLEMENTARY, following further pathological review**

Case reference: Sara Butt (deceased)
Date of examination: 12 June 2025
Supersedes: initial report HP-FS-2025-0203

**Reason for further review:** Given the high public and media profile of this case, a senior pathologist was assigned to conduct a fuller review beyond the initial examination — a level of scrutiny the case would not necessarily have received without that attention. Worth noting plainly: this level of care arrived only after five days' submersion had already compromised much of the evidence: the attention came once the harm was already done, not before it.

**Revised cause of death:** Asphyxiation due to manual strangulation. This supersedes the initial finding of blunt force trauma as the primary mechanism.

**Findings supporting revision:**
- Fracture of the hyoid bone, a skeletal finding largely unaffected by water immersion or the time elapsed before recovery — unlike soft-tissue indicators, which degrade far more readily in these conditions.
- Patterned bruising to the neck consistent with manual compression (a hand grip), distinct in character and location from the bruising elsewhere on the body.
- Petechial haemorrhaging, the classic soft-tissue indicator of strangulation, could not be reliably assessed given the degree of decomposition and submersion — its absence from this report should not be read as absence at the time of death, only as evidence lost to the conditions of recovery.

**Reconciling the initial findings:** The bruising and trauma to the head and body noted in the initial report are now attributed to the physical struggle preceding death and the process of disposal (being dragged and immersed), rather than to a separate fatal blunt-force mechanism. The initial report's conclusion was a reasonable reading of the visible trauma at first examination — it was not an error, given what was available to see at that point, only revised once a fuller review was possible.

**Conclusion:** Death by unlawful killing. Cause of death revised to asphyxiation by manual strangulation.

*Two things deliberately built into this one:*

## Background: Hollenbourne's homicide rate (podcast/framing reference)

For the "murder capital of the UK" framing (used in the intro podcast): 24 murders across an 8-year sample (2018–2025) in a population of ~80,000 — a sustained rate of 3.75 per 100,000/year, roughly 3.75x the national average, and well above any real recent UK town's sustained (not single-year-spike) rate. Distribution built for narration, not evenly spread:

| 2018 | 2 | Early signs of the county lines problem |
| 2019 | 4 | Includes Mason (Oct) |

Each named victim sits inside a year with several other unconnected deaths — none of the four was ever the statistically anomalous case in their own year, which is part of why the pattern went unnoticed at the time.

## The endgame (Week 11, second half — the fun round-up)

*Runs after the mock CW2 assessment earlier the same week, deliberately separate from it — this is unassessed, competitive, and exists to give the term a genuine dramatic payoff. The mock assessment is where data-interpretation and argumentation skills are actually graded; nothing here should be read as academic consequence. Teams are competing for recognition, not marks. **Mechanism: offline and hand-graded, not built into the app.** Students complete a short form (three questions per case: who, what charge, what evidence) and you apply the scoring rubric yourself — see hollenbourne-endgame-form.md for both. No automated scoring logic, no confidence sliders, no in-app submission needed for this part.*

**The core action: "Submit your case."** At the session's close, each team formally names who they believe is responsible for the connected deaths (Mason, Wooley, Butt) and what evidence they're standing on. This is deliberately a single, simple, high-stakes moment rather than another costed action — the drama should come from the decision itself, not from spending points on it.

**Three outcomes, all of them designed to land as something, not just win/lose:**

- **Strong case.** Named Burgess, holding all five core prerequisites (bus records, Paget Street doorbell footage, comparative tool-mark review, property search naming him, vehicle registration). Full reveal: CPS confirms charges proceed, Appendix H (the arrest interview) is read in full. Worth being explicit with students that this isn't a confession-based win — Burgess never confesses, he requests a solicitor — the case rests entirely on accumulated evidence meeting the threshold, which is the realistic and more instructive version of how most prosecutions actually work.

- **Thin case.** Named Burgess correctly, but without the full prerequisite chain — say, three or four of five. CPS declines to charge: insufficient evidence to meet the threshold, even though the belief was right. This is the "correct belief vs. provable case" distinction the whole design has been building toward, and it should be presented to students as exactly that — not a failure of reasoning, a lesson in the gap between knowing and proving. Worth resisting any temptation to soften this into a near-win; the discomfort of being right and still losing is the point.

- **Wrong suspect.** Named someone other than Burgess — realistically Swayne, given Holly Creagan's now-deliberately-ambiguous alibi (procedural documents) gives a team that never thinks to press further a genuine, plausible route to building a case against him instead. This is the institutional-critique landing moment: a team ending the term confidently wrong isn't a game failure, it's the clearest possible demonstration of how profiling and circumstantial reasoning actually produce wrongful suspicion in practice. Not punitive — this outcome should be treated in the room as pedagogically rich, arguably the most interesting one to unpack afterward.

**Awards — recognition for more than just the single "winning" team:**

- **Case Closed** — awarded to the team(s) reaching Strong Case. The headline prize.
- **Sharpest Read** — for a team that named Burgess correctly even on a Thin Case, recognising the reasoning was right even where the evidence gathering fell short.
- **Most Thorough Investigation** — for the team that gathered the most total evidence across the term, independent of their final outcome. Keeps the "wrong suspect" and "thin case" teams from leaving with nothing to show for genuine effort.

*Deliberately no award for speed or point-efficiency alone — the existing action economy already rewards efficient play all term; the endgame's job is to reward good reasoning and thoroughness on the day, not to duplicate that incentive a second time.*
