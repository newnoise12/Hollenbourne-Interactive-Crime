## Stage 1: Reading a single chart

**Context given to students:**

> England and Wales measures crime in two main ways. **Police recorded crime** counts offences that come to police attention and get logged. The **Crime Survey for England and Wales (CSEW)** instead asks a large, representative sample of people directly about their experiences, whether or not they ever reported them to police. Because the CSEW asks people directly, it can tell us what share of crimes actually get reported to the police in the first place — shown in Figure 1.

**[Figure 1 — chart-reporting-rates.png — shown here]**

**Q1. According to Figure 1, approximately what percentage of fraud incidents are reported to the police?**

A) 60%
B) 42%
C) 12%
D) 90%

**Correct: C.** A direct read from the chart — this is the most basic level of the skill, and worth including even though it's simple, since some students will get the harder reasoning questions right while still misreading the chart itself if rushed.

**Q2. Figure 1 shows burglary reported far more often than fraud. Based on what you know about these two crime types, what's the most likely reason for the difference?**

A) Burglary is a more serious crime than fraud, so victims care more
B) Burglary victims usually need a police crime reference number to claim on home insurance, giving a practical reason to report that fraud victims often don't have
C) Fraud is a newer type of crime that police don't take seriously yet
D) There is no real difference — the gap is just random variation in the data

**Correct: B.** This is the actual explanatory mechanism ONS itself gives — not that burglary is inherently more "reportable," but that insurance creates a direct practical incentive that fraud typically doesn't.

**Q3. The "All CSEW-comparable crime" bar shows an average of 42%. Why might it be misleading to treat this single average as representative of "crime" in general?**

A) It isn't misleading — 42% is a perfectly accurate figure for all crime
B) The average hides real variation — some crime types (like burglary) are reported far more, others (like fraud) far less, and a single number flattens that difference
C) Averages are never useful in criminology
D) The CSEW doesn't actually calculate averages

**Correct: B.** This is the harder conceptual point Stage 1 is building toward: a single summary statistic can be technically accurate and still obscure the more important pattern underneath it — exactly the kind of nuance CW2's data interpretation criterion rewards.

## Stage 2: Reading a chart over time

**Context given to students:**

> The gap between recorded and actual crime isn't fixed — it can shrink or grow over time as *police recording practices themselves* change, separately from whether the true amount of crime has changed at all. Figure 2 shows this for violence offences specifically.

**[Figure 2 — chart-recording-rate-over-time.png — shown here]**

**Q4. According to Figure 2, how did the police recording rate for violence offences change between 2014 and 2021?**

A) It fell from 90% to 67%
B) It stayed roughly the same
C) It rose from 67% to 90%
D) The chart doesn't show a time comparison

**Correct: C.** A direct read — recording rates improved substantially over this period.

**Q5. Recorded violence offences rose substantially between 2014 and 2021. Based on Figure 2, what would a careful analyst say about that rise?**

A) It proves violent crime in England and Wales nearly doubled in that period
B) Some — potentially a large share — of the apparent rise reflects police recording more of the violence that was already happening, not necessarily more violence occurring
C) It proves the CSEW is unreliable and should be ignored
D) It has no connection to recording practices at all — the rise is entirely real

**Correct: B.** This is the single hardest and most important reasoning skill this quiz tests: a rise in *recorded* crime is not automatically the same claim as a rise in *actual* crime. Figure 2 gives direct evidence that at least part of any such rise could be a recording-practice effect — without proving the whole rise is fake, which would be swinging just as far wrong in the opposite direction.

**Q6. Someone claims: "This chart proves the CSEW itself is an unreliable measure of crime, since the police figures kept changing under it." What's the flaw in that claim?**

A) There's no flaw — the claim is correct
B) The chart shows the *police recording rate* changing, not the CSEW's own methodology — the CSEW is specifically designed to be unaffected by police recording practices, which is exactly why it can be used to measure the recording rate in the first place
C) The CSEW only started in 2014, so the comparison is meaningless
D) Recording rates and survey reliability are the same thing

**Correct: B.** A good final question because it targets a plausible-sounding but backwards misreading — the CSEW being stable is precisely what makes it useful for *catching* police recording changes, not evidence that it's the unreliable one.

## Stage 3: Bringing it home to Hollenbourne

**Context given to students:**

> Figures 1 and 2 use real national statistics. Figure 3 is different — it's **illustrative data built for this module**, showing what the same "estimated vs recorded" pattern might look like for Hollenbourne specifically, consistent with the town's own case-study material used elsewhere this term. It is not real crime data. Treat it exactly like Figures 1 and 2 for the purpose of practising the skill, but don't cite it as if it were an actual statistic.

**[Figure 3 — chart-hollenbourne-crime-trend.png — shown here]**

**Q7. According to Figure 3, in which year is the gap between the two lines at its narrowest?**

A) 2018
B) 2020
C) 2022
D) 2025

**Correct: D.** A direct read — by 2025 the two lines sit closer together than at any other point on the chart, even though estimated crime is still clearly above recorded crime.

**Q8. Both lines dip sharply in 2020. What's the most likely explanation?**

A) Crime genuinely became far less common everywhere that year for no particular reason
B) 2020 covers the Covid-19 lockdown period, when reduced day-to-day movement and opportunity plausibly reduced many routine crime types
C) The police stopped recording crime altogether that year
D) This is a data error and should be ignored

**Correct: B.** Worth noting this matches a real, well-documented pattern seen nationally during the pandemic — this is one place the illustrative Hollenbourne data is deliberately built to mirror a genuine effect, not invent an implausible one.

**Q9. Between 2022 and 2025, the recorded-crime line stays roughly flat while the estimated-crime line falls. Using the same reasoning as Figure 2, what's a plausible explanation that does *not* assume actual crime is rising?**

A) There is no possible explanation other than crime getting worse
B) Recording practices may have continued improving even as true crime fell, meaning police are now capturing a larger share of a shrinking total — two genuine changes happening at once, partly offsetting each other in the recorded figures
C) The chart must be wrong, since both lines should always move together
D) Recorded crime and estimated crime measure exactly the same thing, so this pattern is impossible

**Correct: B.** This is the same reasoning skill Q5 tested with real data, now applied to a new chart — deliberately repeated so students have to transfer the skill rather than just recall the answer.

**Q10. The case-study material elsewhere in this module notes that 2025 "breaks the improving narrative" for one high-profile type of crime, even though Figure 3 shows overall estimated crime continuing to fall that year. What does this illustrate about the relationship between data and public perception?**

A) The case-study material must be factually wrong, since the chart shows improvement
B) A single serious, high-profile case can dominate how safe a place feels or is reported to be, even while the broader statistical picture is genuinely improving — the two things can both be true at once
C) Aggregate crime data is always more trustworthy than any individual case
D) Individual cases should never be discussed if aggregate data shows improvement

**Correct: B.** The hardest question in the set, and deliberately placed last — it connects directly to Week 8's material on crime representation, and resists the two easy wrong answers (dismiss the data, or dismiss the case) in favour of holding both as genuinely true simultaneously.

## Stage 4: Referencing these sources

*Same addition as the Prisons week quiz, for a consistent approach across the module: "unlocking evidence" turned out not to be a realistic context for referencing, so this is the real one — practising Harvard references for the genuine sources this quiz is built on, plus the same deliberate trap of correctly refusing to cite the one source that isn't real.*

**Q11. Both Figure 1 and Figure 2 are described in this document as coming from "ONS." Why isn't "ONS (2025)" alone an adequate reference for either of them?**

A) It is adequate — the organisation is the only thing that matters
B) ONS publishes many different releases and pieces; without the specific title (e.g. "Crime in England and Wales" vs the diverging-trends methodology piece), a reader can't find which one you actually used
C) ONS should never be cited directly, only secondary sources that quote it
D) The organisation's name should be left out entirely and only the title given

**Correct: B.** This is the practical reason full titles matter, not just a formatting rule: the same organisation publishes dozens of releases, and "ONS (2025)" could mean almost anything. A reader — including whoever's marking your work — needs to be able to find the exact source, not just the publisher.

**Q12. Which of these is the correctly formatted Harvard reference for Figure 2's source?**

A) ONS (2025) *Crime stats*. Online.
B) Office for National Statistics (2024) *Exploring diverging trends between the Crime Survey for England and Wales and police recorded crime*. Available at: [URL] (Accessed: [date]).
C) Exploring diverging trends between the CSEW and police recorded crime, ONS website.
D) Office for National Statistics, no date, diverging trends report.

**Correct: B.** Same five required elements as the Prisons week reference: full organisational name (not the acronym alone), year, full italicised title, "Available at" with a real URL, and an access date. A drops almost everything specific; C has no author position, year, or access structure; D drops the title's actual wording and omits the year entirely.

**Q13. Figure 3 (the Hollenbourne crime trend chart) is explicitly labelled as illustrative, built for this module rather than published by ONS or any real body. If asked to list your sources for an assignment using this quiz, what should you do with Figure 3?**

A) Cite it as ONS (2025), since it's presented in a similar chart style to Figures 1 and 2
B) Don't cite it as an external published source — it isn't one. If you need to reference it at all, label it as illustrative material provided for the module, not attribute it to a body that never produced it
C) Leave it out of your reference list entirely and hope nobody asks
D) Cite it as "Hollenbourne Police (2025)," since that's the fictional force in the case

**Correct: B.** The same judgement tested in the Prisons week quiz, now applied to this module's own case-study material specifically: correctly formatting a reference is worthless if it's applied to a source that was never real. Recognising *when not to cite something as genuine* is the actual skill — silently dropping it (C) avoids the citation-formatting question but doesn't demonstrate the judgement; inventing a plausible-sounding fictional attribution (D) gets the judgement wrong in the opposite direction, treating in-game fiction as if it were a real institutional source.
