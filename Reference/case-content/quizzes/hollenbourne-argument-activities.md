# Argument Formation Activities — Courts/Sentencing Week

*Two activities, same format: students first diagnose a deliberately flawed argument, then build and defend their own on the same question. Deliberately kept clear of the Hollenbourne case itself and of CW2's own resourcing topic — these build out the town's civic and institutional context instead. Both flawed arguments are written to be realistic and superficially persuasive, not obviously silly, so the diagnosis stage does real work. Stage 1 now has a scored multiple-select version alongside the open discussion prompts — suitable for the same automatic trust-bonus mechanism as the other quizzes. Stage 2 (build your own) stays unscored and outside the app, as agreed — a classroom activity, not a points-based one.*

---

## Activity 1: Council regeneration funding

**The question:** Should Hollenbourne council continue investing in Featherton's development, or redirect funds toward regenerating Critchley and Boresfield?

### Stage 1: Diagnose

*Present this argument to students as something a (fictional) councillor has written in a local consultation document, arguing for continued Featherton investment.*

> "Featherton is clearly working. Property values are up, new families are moving into the estate every month, and the Piazza is thriving with new shops opening all the time. Local government should back what's succeeding, not what's failing. Critchley and Boresfield have had years of council attention and investment, and nothing has changed there — the same problems, the same decline. If money hasn't fixed those estates by now, more of it won't either. We should keep investing where investment is clearly paying off, not throw good money after bad."

**Prompts for students (discussion, unscored):**
- What is this argument actually measuring as "success"? Who does that measure include, and who might it leave out?
- The argument claims Critchley and Boresfield have "had years of council attention and investment" — what would you need to check before accepting that claim?
- Is "nothing has changed there" good evidence that investment doesn't work — or could it be evidence of something else entirely (for instance, that investment was too small, too short-term, or never actually happened at the scale claimed)?
- Identify the phrase doing the most emotional work in this argument ("throw good money after bad"). What is it doing instead of providing evidence?

**Scored version — multiple select, "which of these are genuine flaws in the argument above?":**

- ✅ It measures "success" only by property values and new arrivals, without asking who benefits or who might be priced out or left behind
- ✅ "Nothing has changed" is treated as proof that investment doesn't work, without checking how much was actually spent, for how long, or how it compares to what Featherton received
- ✅ "Throw good money after bad" carries strong emotional weight but provides no actual evidence for the claim it's attached to
- ❌ *(distractor)* The argument doesn't include exact pound figures — *not a flaw on its own; an argument can be logically sound without citing precise numbers*
- ❌ *(distractor)* The argument was written by a councillor rather than an independent expert — *not a flaw on its own; who makes an argument doesn't determine whether the argument itself holds up*

*Three genuine flaws, two plausible-but-wrong distractors — deliberately included so the question tests actual diagnosis rather than "select everything that sounds critical."*

### Stage 2: Build

Write your own short argument (150–250 words) on **either side** of this question. Your argument must:
- State your position clearly in the first sentence
- Include at least one piece of evidence or reasoning that isn't just an appeal to fairness or emotion
- Address the strongest point the other side could make, and explain why your position still holds
- Avoid at least one of the flaws you identified in Stage 1

## Activity 2: Force structure

**The question:** Should Hollenbourne keep a dedicated local police presence, or be folded into a wider regional Essex force?

### Stage 1: Diagnose

*Present this argument to students as an extract from a (fictional) regional policing efficiency review, arguing for merging Hollenbourne into a larger regional force.*

> "Crime is crime, wherever it happens. A merged regional force gives officers access to better resources, better technology, and colleagues who've handled a wider range of cases. Small local forces are simply inefficient — duplicating specialist capabilities like forensics or armed response across dozens of small local units wastes taxpayer money that could be spent on frontline policing instead. One larger force means one set of overheads, not fifty. The case for merging is really a case for basic efficiency."

**Prompts for students (discussion, unscored):**
- The argument opens with "crime is crime, wherever it happens." What is this claim assuming, and what might it be leaving out about how local knowledge or community relationships affect policing?
- "Efficiency" is treated here as an unqualified good. What might a force lose in the process of becoming more efficient in this way?
- The argument mentions cost but doesn't mention response times, community trust, or local accountability. Is that a fair omission, or does it stack the argument in one direction?
- Is there a hidden assumption that bigger automatically means better? What would you need to know to test that assumption?

**Scored version — multiple select, "which of these are genuine flaws in the argument above?":**

- ✅ "Crime is crime, wherever it happens" quietly dismisses the value of local knowledge and community relationships without addressing the point at all
- ✅ Treats efficiency as an automatic, unqualified good, without acknowledging anything that might be lost in the process
- ✅ Omits response times, community trust, and local accountability entirely, discussing only cost — a one-sided selection of evidence
- ❌ *(distractor)* The argument mentions saving taxpayer money — *not a flaw on its own; cost is a legitimate factor, the flaw is treating it as the only one that matters*
- ❌ *(distractor)* The argument comes from a "regional policing efficiency review" — *not a flaw on its own; an institutional source doesn't automatically make an argument's reasoning wrong*

*Same shape as Activity 1 deliberately — three genuine flaws, two plausible-but-wrong distractors, so students practise the same diagnostic skill on a different topic rather than a different skill entirely.*

### Stage 2: Build

Write your own short argument (150–250 words) on **either side** of this question. Your argument must:
- State your position clearly in the first sentence
- Include at least one piece of evidence or reasoning that isn't just an appeal to fairness or emotion
- Address the strongest point the other side could make, and explain why your position still holds
- Avoid at least one of the flaws you identified in Stage 1

---

*Both activities use the same Stage 2 success criteria deliberately — the skill being practised (state a position, support it, address the counter-case) is the same skill CW2 grades, so students should recognise the shape of what's being asked of them here when they meet it again in the real assessment.*
