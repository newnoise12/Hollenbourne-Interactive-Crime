# Reoffending and Sentencing Quiz — Prisons Week

*Three parts now, three different skills. Figure 1 is real MOJ data, testing whether students can resist a tempting but wrong causal conclusion — the selection-effects trap. Figure 2 is a deliberately constructed illustrative chart, testing chart-construction literacy rather than data reading. Part 3 adds referencing practice using a genuine text-based government source, plus a deliberate trap: correctly refusing to cite Figure 2 as if it were real. Pairs well with in-class textual analysis of sentencing debates, since all three feed into "is this sentence actually more effective, and how would you know, and how would you properly cite your evidence for it."*

## Part 1: Reoffending by sentence type

**[Figure 1 — chart-reoffending-by-sentence.png — shown here]**

**Q1. According to Figure 1, what is the one-year proven reoffending rate for those given a court order (community or suspended sentence)?**

A) 62%
B) 34%
C) 47%
D) 4%

**Correct: B.** A direct read from the chart.

**Q2. Based only on Figure 1, which of these is the most defensible conclusion?**

A) Court orders cause a lower reoffending rate than short custodial sentences
B) People given court orders reoffend less often than people given short custodial sentences, but the chart alone doesn't tell us why
C) Short custodial sentences have no effect on reoffending at all
D) The two groups are not really comparable, so this chart is worthless

**Correct: B.** This is the honest reading — the chart shows a real, large gap in outcomes, but a bar chart comparing two group averages can't by itself tell you *why* that gap exists. Both A and D go too far in opposite directions: A assumes causation the data can't support alone; D wrongly dismisses real, useful data just because it isn't the whole story.

**Context given to students, before the harder question:**

> Courts don't hand out community orders and short custodial sentences at random. People sentenced to custody are, on average, more likely to have prior convictions, active drug or alcohol problems, or unemployment — the same factors independently linked to reoffending regardless of sentence type. The Ministry of Justice has run its own analysis on exactly this question: using a matched comparison group (people with similar offending history, drug use, and employment status, split across sentence types), the *actual* difference in reoffending attributable to sentence type was around **4 percentage points** — not the 28-point gap Figure 1 shows.

**Q3. Given this, what's the most accurate way to describe what Figure 1 actually demonstrates?**

A) Figure 1 is wrong and should be ignored
B) Figure 1 shows a real difference in outcomes, but most of that gap likely reflects who ends up with each sentence type, not the sentence itself — the matched analysis suggests the sentence's own effect is much smaller (~4 points), though still real and in the same direction
C) The matched analysis proves sentence type makes no difference at all
D) Since experts disagree, no conclusion can be drawn either way

**Correct: B.** This is the whole point of the exercise: neither dismissing the raw chart as meaningless (C, D) nor taking it at face value (the original tempting reading) is correct. The matched analysis doesn't erase the raw finding — it explains *how much* of it is real sentence effect versus pre-existing group differences, and there's still a genuine, if much smaller, effect in the same direction. This is the same reasoning skill as the dark figure of crime quiz's hardest question, now applied to a genuine confound (selection effects) rather than a recording-practice explanation handed to students directly in the text.

## Part 2: Reading a chart's construction, not just its data

**[Figure 2 — chart-dual-axis-illustrative.png — shown here]**

**Context given to students:**

> Figure 2 is a constructed illustration, not a real combined MOJ/ONS publication — built specifically to demonstrate a common chart-construction issue: two different variables, each on its own independently-scaled axis, shown on the same chart.

**Q4. Look carefully at the two axis scales in Figure 2, not just the line shapes. What do you notice?**

A) Both axes use the same scale, so the comparison is fair
B) The crime estimate axis covers a much narrower proportional range relative to how much the actual value changes (nearly halving) than the prison population axis does (changing by under 10%) — yet both lines are drawn to look similarly steep
C) The chart proves prison population and crime are unrelated
D) Nothing unusual — this is a standard, safe way to compare two variables

**Correct: B.** This is the actual construction issue: crime (right axis) falls from 9.5 to 4.6 million — roughly halved — while prison population (left axis) only falls from 85,000 to 79,000 — under 10%. But because each axis is scaled to fill the same vertical space, both lines *look* like they're declining at a similar rate. Reading the line shapes alone, without checking what each axis actually represents, would badly mislead you about the true scale of change in each variable.

**Q5. What's the safest general approach when you encounter a chart with two independently-scaled y-axes?**

A) Trust the visual impression — if two lines look similar, they probably are
B) Ignore the chart entirely — dual-axis charts are always wrong
C) Check both axis labels and ranges specifically before drawing any conclusion about how the two variables relate, since axis scaling is a choice that can create a misleading visual impression even with genuine data
D) Only look at the left axis, since it's usually the more important one

**Correct: C.** Dual-axis charts aren't inherently dishonest — sometimes they're a genuinely useful way to show two related time series together — but the axis scaling is always a choice, and a different choice could tell a visually different story from the same real numbers. The habit worth building is checking the axes before trusting the shape.

## Part 3: Referencing these sources

*This section exists because "unlocking evidence" turned out not to be a realistic context for referencing — you don't cite a piece of evidence you're investigating, you cite a source you're drawing on to make an argument. This is that real context: a third source, presented in a genuinely different format (prose, not a chart), plus practice correctly referencing everything used in this quiz. Same skill taught properly in Week 3; this is the spaced-practice application, in the subject area where it actually matters for CW2.*

**Source 3 — a government bulletin, presented as text rather than a chart:**

> In the 12 months to December 2025, there were 394 deaths in prison custody in England and Wales, an increase of 15% on the previous 12-month period. Of these, 79 were self-inflicted, a decrease of 12% on the previous year's total. Over the same period, the self-harm rate stood at 859 incidents per 1,000 prisoners, a fall of 3.6% year-on-year, while the assault rate rose by 6% to 364 assaults per 1,000 prisoners.
>
> *(Adapted from: Ministry of Justice/HM Prison and Probation Service, Safety in Custody Statistics, England and Wales: Deaths in Prison Custody to December 2025, Self-harm and Assaults to September 2025, published 29 January 2026.)*

**Q7. Which of these is a genuine, immediate observation about Source 3 that a chart couldn't show as directly?**

A) It contains four different statistics in a single paragraph, each with its own comparison point and direction of change, without needing four separate visual elements
B) It proves prisons are becoming less safe overall
C) It's less trustworthy than a chart because it doesn't have an image
D) It can't contain real government statistics, since it's written as prose

**Correct: A.** Worth students noticing explicitly: text and charts aren't ranked by trustworthiness, they're just different formats suited to different amounts and kinds of information — a genuinely mixed picture (some measures up, some down) is often easier to hold precisely in a short paragraph than in a single chart trying to show four trends at once.

**Q8. Which of the following is the correctly formatted Harvard reference for Source 3?**

A) Gov.uk (2026) *Prison Safety Report*. Available online.
B) Ministry of Justice and HM Prison and Probation Service (2026) *Safety in Custody Statistics, England and Wales: Deaths in Prison Custody to December 2025, Self-harm and Assaults to September 2025*. Available at: [URL] (Accessed: [date]).
C) HMPPS Safety in Custody Statistics, seen online, 2026.
D) Ministry of Justice (no date) *Safety in Custody*.

**Correct: B.** A is wrong twice over — "Gov.uk" is the website, not the publishing body, and "Available online" isn't a usable locator. C is missing almost every required element (proper author, full title, formal date/access structure). D drops the actual publication date and the full title, both of which are known and should be included. B has all five things a Harvard web reference needs: correct organisational author, year, full italicised title, "Available at" with a real URL, and an access date.

**Q9. Figure 2 (the prison population/crime dual-axis chart from Part 2) is explicitly labelled as illustrative, not a genuine combined publication. If you were writing an assignment and wanted to reference where Figure 2 came from, what should you do?**

A) Reference it the same way as Figure 1 and Source 3, since it's a chart with real-looking numbers on it
B) Don't cite it as an external source at all — since it isn't one, the honest thing is to label it as your own illustrative construction (or your tutor's, if given to you that way), not attribute it to a body that never published it
C) Attribute it to the Ministry of Justice, since the chart is about prisons
D) Leave the source unclear, since it doesn't really matter for a chart

**Correct: B.** This is the actual point of including a fabricated source in the set: citation mechanics are useless if applied to the wrong judgement. Getting the Harvard format perfect for a source that was never real is worse than getting a real source's format slightly wrong — the first mistake is invisible to the exact skill this quiz is teaching, and the second is exactly what practice is for.
